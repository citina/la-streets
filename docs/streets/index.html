      How this was measured, and what it can't tell you

      Data

      LADOT parking citations (data.lacity.org, dataset 4f5p-udkv) from the last two years, rebuilt every week.

      Blocks and sides

      Each ticket's address is matched to the City of Los Angeles street centerlines (LA GeoHub), which list the house numbers on each side of every stretch of street. A block is one street's hundred block, and its line on the map is the part of the street those numbers cover. Tickets written at an intersection, like "5th St / Wall St", have no house number and are left out, and so are streets outside the City of Los Angeles. The centerlines also say which side of the street has the even numbers, so the card names the compass side.

      Sweeping schedule

      The posted day, weeks and time come from StreetsLA's map of posted sweeping routes. Each route covers an area and is swept on two days, one for each side of the street. A side gets the route day that matches the weekday of most of its sweeping tickets, among the routes those tickets sit inside. A side needs at least 8 sweeping tickets for that. The card also counts how many of a side's sweeping tickets on its sweep day fell in its posted weeks, since signs often show only the day. A few routes, like Downtown's, are swept every week; one with no fixed time is left out. Tickets written outside the posted time aren't counted. When neither side is matched, the card lists the posted route days around the block instead (tested a few metres to each side of the street), without saying which side gets which, or says that no posted route covers it. "Tickets were written on X% of sweep days" counts posted days with at least one ticket, leaving out holidays and any day with no sweeping tickets anywhere in the city.

      Meter patrols

      Monday to Saturday, 8 am to 8 pm, holidays left out. A visit is a group of meter tickets on one block with no gap over 10 minutes. An officer who finds every meter paid leaves no ticket, so the visit rate is adjusted for those empty visits, assuming the number of expired cars an officer finds on a visit is random (Poisson). A block needs at least 20 meter tickets. Which blocks have meters comes from LADOT's meter inventory (dataset s49e-q6j2).

      Ticket kinds

      LADOT's handheld devices write the same rule in different ways, like "STANDNG IN ALLEY" or "8069B NO PARK ST CLN", so tickets are grouped by violation code under one plain name. The no-stopping codes, including the rush-hour ones, all become "No stopping", and the usual hours show when they're written. Fines are the median of the tickets that list one. Tapping a kind shows what the rule is and the ticket code LADOT writes most often for it. For the block's most ticketed kind, and any kind with at least 50 tickets there, it also opens a dot chart of the half hour each ticket was written.

      Limits

      A ticket only appears when someone broke the rule, so quiet blocks look less enforced than they are, and a rule nobody was ticketed for doesn't show up at all. Addresses that couldn't be matched are left out. Routes and staffing change, and none of this is a promise about next week. Always read the posted signs, and for sweeping, the city's schedule.

      About These pages

      Made by Citina Liang, a PhD candidate in Industrial & Systems Engineering at USC Viterbi who models how people behave and how diseases spread. After she missed the deadline for a campus parking pass, she built Curb Log to see when the meters near her office have space, then USC Ticket Clock and LA Street Rules to learn when enforcement comes, near campus and across LA. All from the city's open data, updated every week. Built with Claude Code in about a week; the code is on GitHub.

      Not a guide to parking illegally. This page doesn't suggest parking anywhere, or at any time, the rules don't allow. It shows when and where LADOT has written tickets, so the rules on each block and how they're enforced are easier to understand. Past tickets don't predict the next one: an officer can come by at any time, and a quiet hour in the data can still end in a ticket.

      What the page loads. The map images come from OpenStreetMap and the block data from this site, which GitHub hosts, one small area at a time, so both can tell roughly which part of the city you're looking at. If you use your location, it only moves the map; the exact spot isn't sent anywhere.
